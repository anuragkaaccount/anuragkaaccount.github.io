The form analyser

How the mirror measures you.

A webcam alone sees in 2D. It can't tell how deep a squat goes or whether your knees drift toward it. So I fused it with LiDAR and worked out the maths that turns pixels into millimetres, angles and a form score, then used AI to turn that maths into fast, working code.

01 · Scale

Calibrate to your body

mm/px = Hyou ÷ hpx

Your real height (from onboarding) divided by your height in pixels, found by edge detection. No guessing the camera's field of view.

02 · Verify

Cross-check the camera

FOVv = 2·atan( h ÷ 2d )

Back-calculates the camera's vertical field of view from LiDAR depth. Three methods vote; more than 5% disagreement triggers a recalibration.

03 · Depth

Find you in the scan

d = median( rθ ), θ ∈ [−15°, +15°]

The LiDAR spins 360°. I take the median distance in a 30° cone in front of the mirror, averaged over the last 5 scans.

04 · Clean

Remove the jitter

6 Hz Butterworth low-pass · fill if vis < 0.5

Raw joints shake. A low-pass filter smooths every joint, and joints the camera briefly loses are filled from their neighbours.

05 · Angles

Measure every joint

θ = arccos( BA·BC ÷ |BA||BC| )

The angle at each joint from the vectors to its neighbours, in real 3D millimetres once depth is fused in.

06 · Classify

Know the exercise

22 features → BiLSTM → label

Beyond rules: a bidirectional LSTM reads a window of 22 movement features and names the exercise. Tested in the standalone analyser.

Counting reps: a state machine

Knee angle below 100° means you're down. Back above 160° means one rep. Nothing in between counts, so half-reps and wobbles don't.

Scoring form: 100, minus mistakes

Every frame starts at 100 and loses points for each rule broken. Form % is the average of the last 20 frames. These are the squat rules.

Not deep enough (knee > 110° at the bottom)−15
Slight forward lean (torso > 40°)−10
Back rounding (torso > 50°)−25
Knees caving (knee gap ÷ hip gap < 0.7)−30
Hip shift (> 8°)−15

Why the extra sensor was worth it

Depth turns a form score from a guess into a measurement. Squat depth and knee travel are exactly the errors that injure people, and exactly what 2D can't see.

Trade-off: one more sensor, its own 5 V supply, and calibration. The LiDAR sits at the camera's height, 85–90 cm off the floor.

Decision · three voices, one rule

Problem

When the system and the coach share one voice, people can't tell setup from training. Is this a menu or my trainer?

● The call

Three voices, one rule. Kria, a blue orb, is the system and only speaks at first launch. Aryan, a gold orb, is the coach. Tara is the alternative coach.

Trade-off

Two characters to keep consistent in every line, written and generated. The orb always shows idle, listening or speaking.

The Kria system

Proven kettlebell science. My system around it.

The daily practice is built on established kettlebell training. What I designed is everything that makes it adapt: the periodization, the progression rules and a plan that bends instead of breaking.

1 fast lift

An explosive lift for power and conditioning.

1 slow lift

A slow, controlled lift for strength and mobility.

< 30 min

One kettlebell. Every day. Built to be a habit, not an event.

8-week periodized block

Volume comes down as the bell gets heavier. Weeks 4 and 8 are lighter (half volume, same load) so the body consolidates instead of breaking down.

3-bell progression, gated by form

You only move up a bell when your form holds at 85% or more for three sessions in a row. Strength follows technique.

Interaction design

Designed for glass, not a screen.

Black disappears. Light survives.

Behind a two-way mirror, black pixels become your reflection. So the UI is near-black, with warm off-white type and one gold accent. Red means stop. Type is sized for 1–2 metres, and the data sits at the edges at mid-height so your body stays clear in the middle and you can check your own form.

Trade-off

No full-bleed imagery mid-workout. Everything has to read through glass in a lit room.

Voice first. Glance second.

You're up to two metres away, holding 16 kg, sweating. Touch is out. So every screen runs on voice, and every button is labelled with exactly what you say, so the label teaches the command. Four commands per screen, max.

Silence counts as an answer: Plan Review moves on after 3 seconds, Recap after 15.

A tiny vocabulary. Deeper features live in Studio, Journey and Profile, never on a workout screen.

Building it

Designed by me. Accelerated by AI.

No team, no dev hand-off. I sketched and cut the steel frame in the NID workshop and set two-way glass over a portrait display. The idea, the interaction design, the system logic and the maths are mine. AI was my execution layer: I briefed it the way I would brief an engineering team, reviewed what came back, tested it on the real mirror and kept only what met the bar. That is how one designer shipped like a small team.

What I'd do next

01

Validate the form analyser with experts

Today's form rules are hand-tuned thresholds. Next, certified kettlebell coaches and physiotherapists grade recorded reps, and I tune the maths until the mirror agrees with them.

02

Build an expert-labelled dataset

Graded reps become training data, so the model moves from naming the exercise to judging how well it was done.

03

Measure the machine

Frame rate, voice latency and rep accuracy against hand counts. The prototype works; next it needs numbers.

04

Then, real homes

Five homes, two weeks each. Measure days trained per week, not calories. That's the number that proves "daily practice".

What I learned

A mirror is designed by absence.

The best interface here is the one you stop noticing. Every pixel had to earn its place against your own reflection.

Voice UX is mostly timing.

When to speak, when to wait, when silence means yes. Getting the pauses right mattered more than the words.

Building it changed the design.

Glare, distance, latency and sweat rewrote decisions that looked finished on a laptop screen.
